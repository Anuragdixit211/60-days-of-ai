Headline

2/10Recruiter's 3-second reaction: skips straight past it
❌ The default trapMost students leave the default LinkedIn headline: "MCA Student at [College name]". That's it. No skill, no direction, no reason to click.
🧠 Why it hurtsRecruiters searching for candidates type skills into LinkedIn search — "Java developer," "backend intern," "SQL fresher." A headline with only your degree and college name never surfaces in those searches. You become invisible before anyone even opens your profile.
🔍 Invisible costYou never find out you were "seen" — because you weren't. There's no rejection to learn from, just silence. That silence gets misread as "my skills aren't good enough" when the real problem is nobody could find you.

About — first 2 lines

1/10"See more" never gets clicked
❌ The default trapLeft blank, or opens with "I am a final year MCA student passionate about technology and looking for opportunities to grow." Every single word of that sentence is filler a recruiter has read 10,000 times.
🧠 Why it hurtsLinkedIn cuts your About section after ~2 lines with a "see more" button. If those 2 lines don't say something specific, recruiters never click through — meaning your actual story, projects, and skills are never read, no matter how good they are.
🔍 Invisible costThis is the single highest-leverage fix on your entire profile. A generic opening line silently kills 90% of your profile's real content before anyone gets to it.

Skills & keywords

4/10Shows up in some searches, not the ones that matter most
❌ The default trapYou listed C++, Java, Python, SQL, DBMS — genuinely solid, in-demand skills. The trap isn't the skills, it's stopping there: no framework, no project-specific tools, no ordering by priority.
🧠 Why it hurtsRecruiters filter search results by top-listed skills first. If your top 3 pinned skills aren't the ones matching the roles you want, you rank lower in results even though you technically "have" the right skill somewhere on the list.

Building MediGenie taught me something no DBMS lecture did: a "correct" search result and a "useful" one aren't the same thing. Early on, my search just matched keywords. Technically correct, practically useless — users didn't want the closest string match, they wanted the most relevant suggestion. Fixing that meant thinking about ranking, not just retrieval. Small shift, completely changed how I approach every project since. If you're a student building search or recommendation features: don't stop at "it returns results." Ask if it returns the RIGHT results.
